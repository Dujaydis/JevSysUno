01 · The core idea

It is a classifier wearing a language model's clothes

Normal AI models write text. You ask a question, they type out an answer one word at a time, and your code has to read that answer and try to make sense of it. Sometimes they make things up. Sometimes they reply in a format your code can't read, and everything breaks.

Jev doesn't write anything. Instead you tell it, in advance, exactly what the possible answers are — "either billing, technical, or other" — and it tells you how likely each one is. It literally has no way to answer with anything else.

Think of it like the difference between an essay question and a multiple-choice bubble sheet. Jev only fills in bubbles. It's much faster and much cheaper, and it can never scribble outside the lines. But it also can never explain itself.

A conventional LLM is autoregressive: it samples one token at a time, and the "structure" in structured output is a grammar constraint bolted onto that sampling loop. Jev has no sampling loop. It is a closed-set classification and scoring head — a single forward pass that produces probabilities over an answer set you defined before the call.

This is why the marketing can say it "mathematically cannot hallucinate," and why that claim is narrower than it sounds. There is no string being generated, so there is no string that can be malformed. The guarantee is about the shape of the answer, and it is airtight. It says nothing whatsoever about whether the answer is right.

The most useful one-liner — which TypeSafe's own CEO reportedly agreed with on launch day — is simply: it's a very good zero-shot classifier with a typed interface.

Architecturally you are calling something much closer to argmax over a fixed logit set than to a text model. The name is a Kahneman reference: System 1 is fast, intuitive judgment made without deliberation — as opposed to System 2's slow, effortful reasoning. The positioning is that most decisions inside software were never System 2 problems in the first place, and paying frontier-LLM latency and cost for them is waste.

Every property of the product, good and bad, falls out of the single-forward-pass-over-a-closed-set design:

What you gain

  • No parse step, no repair prompt, no retry-on-malformed-JSON — an entire bug class disappears.
  • One pass, so it's fast. Independently measured: 777 judgments in under 0.7 s.
  • Extra questions in the same request are nearly free in latency — they evaluate in parallel.
  • You get the whole distribution, which is what makes a real escalation path possible.

What you give up

  • No explanation, rationale or citation. It cannot tell you why.
  • One reasoning step. Multi-hop inference is out of scope.
  • No retrieval — everything it needs must be in the state you send.
  • Questions in one request cannot see each other's answers.
  • The answer space must be fully known in advance.

That last constraint is the sharpest one in practice. If you cannot enumerate the answers before the call, Jev is not a candidate technology — not "harder to use," but structurally inapplicable.

The asymmetry that trips people up: Choice and Score return a confidence field. Noul does not — and that is deliberate, not an oversight. For a yes/no question the probability is the uncertainty. Asking "how confident are you in that 0.5?" is a category error: 0.5 already means "genuinely torn."

  


  

    
Choosing between them

    

      
You needUseThe distinction that matters

      
Whether a condition holdsnoulNo separate confidence. If several labels can be true at once, use one Noul per label — not a Choice.

      
One of a defined setchoiceThe distribution compares competing options. Include a no-match outcome when nothing may fit.

      
Degree along a dimensionscoreReturns a probability-weighted position, not a bucket. Levels must describe concrete situations and stand alone.

    

    
Two shape rules the SDK enforces, because both are easy to get backwards: choice criteria are a map of label → description, and score criteria are an ordered list where the index is the level, starting at zero. Pass a list to Choice or a map to Score and it throws before the request leaves your process.

  







  
03 · The trap

  
A Score of 1.5 does not mean what you think

  
score is not the most likely level. It is the probability-weighted average of the levels — an expected value. Which means two completely different situations produce the identical number.

Both cases return score: 1.5. One is a model calmly saying "between level 1 and 2." The other is a model flipping a coin between "trivial" and "critical." If you branch on score alone, those are indistinguishable — and one of them will page someone at 3am for nothing, or fail to page them at all.

    
When the difference matters to your logic, read probabilities, not score. That is what it's there for.

  







  
04 · The evidence

  
It is not more accurate. That is not the pitch.


  
The marketing says Jev is much faster and much cheaper than a normal AI model. That part is true. But people outside the company ran their own tests, and on one realistic task Jev was noticeably worse at getting the right answer than a small ordinary model. Speed and price are the reason to use it — not accuracy.


  

    
Two independent benchmarks were published with real hand-labelled ground truth. They disagree with each other, and that disagreement is the single most important thing on this page.

Same model, same week, opposite verdicts. Calibration quality is a property of your task, not of the product. There is no way to know which case you are in without measuring on your own data — which is exactly why an eval harness is the first thing to build, not the last.

    

  


  

    
Why the vendor's own numbers can't settle it

    
TypeSafe's headline benchmark reports Jev at 67.8% against a frontier model's 67.9% — near parity. But the "reference answers" it is graded against were produced by averaging two other frontier LLMs, not by human labelling.

    
That measures "does Jev answer the way two big models answer." It does not measure correctness, and it structurally caps Jev at "agrees with the consensus of models that may themselves be wrong." Launch-day commentary flagged this as the top methodological concern. Do not inherit this methodology when you build your own evaluation.

    
Worth stating plainly: no number found anywhere — including TypeSafe's own — shows Jev being more accurate than the models it is priced against. Best case in vendor material is parity.


    
So what is the real case for it?

    
Latency, cost, and an interface that cannot break. Those are substantial and real: they make judgment economically viable at volumes where an LLM call per item is absurd, and they delete the parse-and-repair bug class outright. Adopt Jev for those reasons, with eyes open. Do not adopt it believing it is more correct, because the only independent head-to-head says the opposite.

  







  
05 · The pattern that makes it safe

  
Three bands, and the thresholds live in your code

  
Because you get a probability rather than a bare answer, you can route by how sure the model is: act automatically when it's confident, ask a human when it isn't. The thresholds belong in your code — TypeSafe ships no threshold config, and that is the correct design.

The lesson the slider teaches: an identical threshold policy produces a safe system under one calibration regime and a quietly broken one under the other. You cannot pick thresholds from a blog post, a cookbook, or this page. You pick them from a reliability diagram built on your own labelled data — and you re-check them whenever the model version moves.

  


  

    
The alias trap

    
jev-latest is an alias, and an alias moves when a new version ships. Your answers — and the calibration you so carefully measured — can change with no change on your side.

    
Pin the concrete version in production via GET /v1/models, and treat a version bump as a change that re-runs the whole eval harness. Pin your question schema alongside it: a question's wording and the model answering it are a matched pair and only make sense evaluated together.

  







  
06 · The decision

  
Should you use it?

  
Jev is a good fit when you're sorting things into known buckets, at high volume, where speed and cost matter more than getting every last case right — and where a human can check the uncertain ones. It's a bad fit for anything that needs writing, explaining, or multi-step thinking.


  

    
Answer yes to most of these, or use something else

    
    
      
  • Is this a judgment, not a creation?
    • 
      
  • Is the answer space definable in advance?
    • 
      
  • Is it one atomic decision, not a chain?
    • 
      
  • Does all the context it needs fit in state you can supply?
    • 
      
  • Could a human expert judge it in seconds?
    • 
      
  • Will software consume the result directly?
    • 
    

    
Zero to two yeses means a different technology. And before adopting anything, ask the sharper question: is calibration actually my bottleneck? If a simple margin threshold on what you already have works, adding a vendor buys you nothing.


    
Walk away when

    
    
      
  • You must show a user why. It cannot produce a rationale. Full stop.
    • 
      
  • The task is rule-expressible. A regex beat it on the phishing task — at zero cost, zero latency, zero vendor risk.
    • 
      
  • The decision is multi-hop. One reasoning step is the design.
    • 
      
  • The data is regulated. There is no on-prem or VPC option; state leaves your perimeter with no configuration that prevents it.
    • 
    

  


  

    
Risks worth naming out loud

    

      
RiskReality as of 18 Sep 2026

      
MaturityThree days old. No named production customers, no outage history, no pricing history. Absence of bad history at three days is not evidence of stability.

      
Lock-inClosed weights, API-only, single vendor. No second provider serves this interface, so "switch providers" is not an available mitigation — the exit is rewriting the decision layer.

      
DataAll state leaves your infrastructure. Privacy terms could not be verified in this research.

      
AvailabilityNo SLA identified. Rate limits reported as "adjusting dynamically without notice." Treat 429 as normal operation, not an exception.

      
Stability2.2% of identical repeated inputs returned a different label. Answers are not deterministic.

    

    
The mitigations that actually work: keep question definitions, thresholds and policy as data in your own code so only transport is vendor-specific; build the cheap local baseline first; redact before building state, since Jev can only ever see exactly what you hand it; and pin the model version.

  







  
07 · How we know this

  
Where these facts came from

  
typesafe.ai and docs.typesafe.ai were unreachable from the research environment. Rather than guess, the research routed through GitHub, which was reachable. Everything on this page carries a confidence tier.


  

    
Tier 1  First-party source code, read in full. TypeSafe's official Python and JS SDKs, cloned from their GitHub org. The Python wire models are generated by datamodel-codegen from their real openapi.json, so every field name and type is exact. All API details on this page come from here.

    
Tier 2  Independent measurement, read in full. The two benchmark repositories, with real hand-labelled ground truth. All accuracy and calibration numbers come from here.

    
Tier 3  Search summaries of pages that could not be opened. The vendor blog, the docs site, Hacker News, the review articles. Useful for knowing what exists; not quotable. Nothing from this tier was allowed into code.

  


  

    
Never hard-code these — none were confirmed

    
Pricing (the widely-repeated $0.042/M input figure), rate limits, context window, max questions per request, the version string behind jev-latest, and "RLCD" as an official term. All Tier 3. One such claim — "no automatic retries" — is flatly contradicted by the SDK source, which defaults to two.

    
The one pricing fact that is first-party is the OpenAPI field description on usage.output_tokens: "Output tokens are currently free of charge."

  


  

    
On the "community" around Jev: search surfaced at least seven near-identical awesome-jev repositories under unrelated accounts, created the same week with identical boilerplate, plus verbatim mirrors of the official SDK under personal accounts. That pattern reads as generated repo spam, not adoption. If you go looking for examples yourself, check provenance before trusting anything.
